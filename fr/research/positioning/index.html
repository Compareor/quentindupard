Le test des trois entreprises pour le positionnement

Positioning 5 min read Quentin Dupard Updated August 2026

Here is a test that takes two minutes and tends to ruin an afternoon.

Open your homepage. Read the headline and the sentence under it. Now ask whether that copy could plausibly describe three other companies in your category.

If it could, you do not have positioning. You have a description, and no amount of paid spend will fix it. You will simply be paying to confuse people at a higher velocity.

Why vague copy happens

Nobody sets out to write generic positioning. It arrives through entirely reasonable decisions, made one at a time.

Someone worries a specific claim will exclude a segment, so it gets softened. Someone else notes that a competitor uses a particular phrase and it seems safe. A large customer asks for a feature and it earns a mention on the homepage. Repeat for two years and you arrive at a page that offends nobody and persuades nobody.

A positioning statement that excludes nobody is a description, not a position.

The three questions

1. Who is it for, stated specifically enough to exclude people?

"Teams of all sizes" is not an answer. "Finance teams at companies with more than one legal entity" is. Specificity feels risky because it is visibly turning some people away. That is the point: the people who remain recognise themselves immediately, and recognition is what converts.

2. What does it replace?

Every purchase displaces something. A spreadsheet, an agency, a contractor, a competitor, or a painful manual process someone currently owns. If you cannot name what dies when a customer buys you, there is no budget line, and the deal will stall in procurement while everyone insists they are still interested.

3. Why now?

Without urgency, a rational buyer defers. Deals then die at the final stage and teams misdiagnose it as a closing problem, which leads to sales training that does not help. The urgency has to be real: a regulation, a contract renewal, a cost that grows, a risk that compounds. Manufactured urgency is transparent and expensive.

Fixing it is a writing problem

Positioning does not get fixed in a workshop. It gets fixed by someone sitting down and writing a specific claim that a reasonable person could disagree with, then defending it.

The useful raw material is not in a brainstorm. It is in your closed-won notes and your churn interviews. What did people actually say when they bought, and what did they say when they left? The language is usually already there, in the words customers used, and it is almost never the language on the homepage.

A quick way to test a new line

Write the new positioning, then run the three-company test on it again. Then show it to someone who has never seen your product and ask them two questions: who is this for, and what would they stop doing if they bought it?

If they can answer both without hedging, you have something. If they say "it sounds like it could work for lots of businesses", you have written another description, and it is worth going back.

Once the positioning is specific, what you charge becomes a much easier argument — price is read as evidence of the claim you are making.

Questions fréquentes

How do I know if my positioning is weak?

Read your homepage headline and the sentence under it, then ask whether that copy could plausibly describe three other businesses in your category. If it could, you have a description rather than a position.

What makes positioning specific enough?

It has to exclude people. Name who it is for precisely, name what it replaces when someone buys, and name why now. A positioning statement that turns nobody away persuades nobody either.

Can a workshop fix positioning?

Rarely. Positioning gets fixed by writing a specific claim someone could disagree with and defending it. The raw material is in your closed-won notes and churn interviews, in the words customers actually used.
