Votre CAC augmente parce que vous avez épuisé le bon segment

Acquisition 6 min read Quentin Dupard Updated August 2026

When acquisition cost climbs, the instinct is to blame the channel, the creative, or the sales team. Occasionally that is right. Usually the market is telling you something less comfortable: you have used up the people who were easy to sell to.

Every business has a best-fit segment where the product obviously solves an obvious problem. Those deals close fast, at good prices, with short sales cycles. They also run out. What happens next looks exactly like performance decay, because you are now paying to reach people who were always going to be harder.

The blended number is hiding the story

Aggregate CAC is an average across segments that behave nothing alike. It tells you the temperature of a building with one room on fire.

Split closed-won deals by company size and use case, then calculate payback period for each. The picture is usually stark: one segment paying back inside nine months, another taking two years or more, and a blended figure that looks tolerable while concealing both.

Every hour a rep spends on a thirty-month payback deal is stolen from a nine-month one.

What the segments are telling you

  • Payback under 12 months. Healthy. Spend more here. This is where your growth budget belongs.
  • 12 to 18 months. Workable, but watch it. If it is drifting upward quarter on quarter, the segment is saturating.
  • Over 18 months with a human seller involved. The economics do not support the motion. Either these deals move to self-serve or you stop chasing them.

That last one is the uncomfortable conclusion, and it is usually the correct one. Slow-payback segments do not need better salespeople. They need to cost less to serve, which means removing the human from the transaction.

Running both motions

You do not "switch" from sales-led to product-led. You run both and let the maths decide which owns which segment.

The common failure is bolting a free tier onto an enterprise product, watching thousands of signups convert at near zero, and concluding that product-led growth does not work. It was never product-led growth. The product had no moment where one person, alone, without a colleague or an admin or an integration, got something useful. Without that moment there is nothing for a self-serve motion to stand on.

Self-serve is not a pricing page. It is a product that works for one person on their first afternoon.

Before you spend more

  1. Segment closed-won by size and use case. Calculate payback for each, not blended.
  2. Check whether your best segment is still growing or whether you have taken most of it.
  3. Look at win rate over time within each segment. Falling win rate in a segment you used to dominate means saturation, not execution.
  4. Ask whether the slow segment could ever be served without a human. If yes, build that. If no, stop paying to reach them.

None of this is a reason to spend less on marketing. It is a reason to know which segment you are spending it on, which is a different and much cheaper problem to fix.

Questions fréquentes

Why is my customer acquisition cost going up?

Most often because you have exhausted your best-fit segment and are now paying to reach people who were always going to be harder to convert. The blended number hides this. Segmenting closed-won by size and use case usually makes it obvious.

What is a good CAC payback period?

Under twelve months is healthy for most subscription businesses. Past eighteen months in your smallest segment is the market telling you those deals cannot carry a human seller, and that segment needs a self-serve path or should be dropped.

Should I switch from sales-led to product-led growth?

You do not switch. You run both and let payback period decide which motion owns which segment. Keep humans where payback still works and move the slow segment to self-serve.
