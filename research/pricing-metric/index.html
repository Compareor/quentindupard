Your pricing metric matters more than your price

Pricing 6 min read Quentin Dupard Updated August 2026

Almost every pricing conversation I get pulled into starts with the wrong question. People ask whether they should charge more. The better question is whether they are charging for the right thing at all.

Your billing metric is the unit you charge against. Per seat, per contact, per gigabyte, per transaction, per site, flat fee. It is picked early, usually by two or three people, usually because it was the easiest thing to count. Then it never gets revisited, because changing it feels like open heart surgery on the business.

It gets revisited far less often than it should, and it is quietly deciding your ceiling.

The compounding test

As a customer succeeds wildly with your product, does your invoice grow?

That is the whole test. If a customer triples their usage, triples their team, triples the value they get, and still pays you the same amount, you have built a business that must keep winning new logos just to stand still.

Per-seat pricing is the most common offender. It is popular because it is easy to bill and easy to forecast, not because it reflects value. It works when value genuinely scales with headcount: a communication tool, a CRM, anything where more people using it means more benefit delivered.

It stops working the moment your product gets more valuable through usage rather than users. An analytics tool where one analyst processes ten times the data is delivering ten times the value on the same invoice. You have handed your best customers a volume discount they did not ask for and did not negotiate.

Signs your metric is wrong

  • Your biggest customers are your least profitable. Support load and infrastructure cost scale with usage; your revenue does not.
  • Customers game the metric. Sharing logins is not a security problem, it is a pricing problem announcing itself.
  • Sales keeps discounting the same line item. If reps routinely knock seats off to close, the metric does not match how the buyer thinks about value.
  • Expansion revenue is flat despite happy customers. Retention is fine, nobody spends more. There is no axis along which success costs them anything.

Changing it without breaking everything

The fear is that repricing triggers a churn event. In practice it rarely does, because you are almost never repricing existing customers.

The sequence that works:

  1. New customers first. Change the model for new business only. You learn whether the market accepts it with zero risk to existing revenue.
  2. Grandfather generously and loudly. Tell existing customers they are on legacy pricing indefinitely. This costs you very little and buys enormous goodwill.
  3. Migrate at natural moments. Renewals, upgrades, contract changes. Never send a mass email announcing everyone is moving.
  4. Keep a hybrid if you need one. A platform fee plus a usage component is often the honest answer and is easier to sell than pure usage, which buyers dislike because it is unpredictable.

Pricing is also positioning. What you charge tells the market what you are, faster and louder than any messaging exercise.

The easiest experiment nobody runs

If you want a single, low-risk thing to try this quarter: raise prices by ten to fifteen percent for new customers only. Existing accounts are untouched, so there is no churn risk. Watch win rate for a month.

Most teams find win rate does not move measurably, which tells you something important. You were leaving that margin on the table the entire time, and it flows straight to the bottom line because it costs nothing to deliver.

If win rate does drop sharply, that is also valuable. It means you are priced at the edge of what your positioning supports, and the constraint is not the number. It is what people believe they are buying — which is a positioning problem, not a pricing one.

Common questions

How do I know if my pricing metric is wrong?

Apply the compounding test: as a customer succeeds wildly with what you sell, does their invoice grow? If not, the metric caps your revenue. Other signs are your biggest customers being your least profitable, customers sharing logins, and sales repeatedly discounting the same line item.

Is per-seat pricing bad?

Only when value does not scale with headcount. Per-seat works for communication and collaboration tools where more people genuinely means more benefit. It fails when the product gets more valuable through usage, data volume or transactions, because it hands your heaviest users a volume discount nobody negotiated.

What is the lowest-risk way to raise prices?

Apply the increase to new customers only. Existing accounts are untouched, so there is no churn risk, and you learn whether the market accepts it within a month by watching win rate.
