Most roadmaps are a list of things nobody would cancel

Product strategy 5 min read Quentin Dupard Updated August 2026

Deciding what to build is the fun part and everyone wants a say. Deciding what to kill is the part that actually shapes a product, and almost nobody is incentivised to do it.

Look at most roadmaps and you will find a list of things nobody was willing to cancel. Each item has a person attached, a history, a meeting where it was agreed. Removing it means telling someone their thing does not matter. So the default is accumulation, and the product gets slower, harder to explain and harder to sell.

Bloat is an incentive problem

Nobody gets promoted for the feature they did not ship. Adding is visible, attributable and safe. Removing is invisible, contentious and slightly career-risky. Given those incentives, any team will accumulate, regardless of how good its process is.

This is why roadmap bloat does not respond to better prioritisation frameworks. You cannot score your way out of a problem that is fundamentally about who has to say no to whom.

If nobody on the team can name who asked for it and what they will stop doing once it ships, it should not be on the roadmap.

Three questions per item

Who specifically asked for this?

Not "customers". A name. If the honest answer is one loud account, or an executive who mentioned it once, that is worth knowing before it consumes a quarter. One customer asking loudly is not demand, it is a sample size of one with good timing.

What will they stop doing once it exists?

Real demand displaces something: a spreadsheet, a manual process, a competitor, a contractor. If nothing gets displaced, the feature is a nice-to-have, and nice-to-haves do not get adopted, which means they do not get renewed on either.

Is this here because of evidence or because of effort already spent?

Sunk cost is the most common reason a doomed item survives a planning meeting. The work already done is gone whether you ship it or not. The only question is whether the remaining work is the best use of the next quarter.

The work you have already done is not an argument for finishing it.

Killing things without a committee

  1. List everything in flight and everything committed. Include the quiet items nobody discusses.
  2. Run the three questions above on each. Do it fast; the obvious cases are obvious.
  3. Separate "kill" from "not now". Not-now items go on a list with a date to revisit, which makes cancellation politically survivable.
  4. Announce what you killed and why. Silent cancellation breeds the belief that things get quietly dropped, which makes people fight harder for their items next time.

The reason outside help is often useful here is not insight. It is that an outsider can say the thing everyone privately agrees with and absorb the social cost of saying it. That is frequently worth more than the analysis.

Once the roadmap is honest, the next question is usually whether you are charging correctly for what survives.

Common questions

How do I decide what to remove from a product roadmap?

Ask what each item is really there for. Anything on the roadmap because of one loud customer, an executive's preference, or sunk effort rather than evidence of demand is a candidate. If nobody can name who asked for it and what they will stop doing once it ships, it should not be there.

Why do product roadmaps get bloated?

Because adding is socially easy and removing is socially expensive. Every item has someone attached to it, so the default is accumulation. Bloat is an incentive problem, not a planning problem.

Should I remove features nobody uses?

Usually yes, but check whether low usage means low value or poor discovery first. A feature used by 2% of accounts that are your highest-paying accounts is not a candidate for removal.

An outside opinion is often the cheapest way to cancel something everyone privately wants cancelled.
